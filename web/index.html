The Story So Far

The Exclusion Zone held for eleven days. On the twelfth, the Army fell back across the river and blew the bridges behind them, and every radio in Knox County hissed into static at the same minute — like the world outside had simply hung up.

The ones who lived called themselves survivors. The ones who organized called themselves Goons. Nobody remembers who said it first — some say it was scratched into the water tower in Muldraugh under a tally of kills — but it stuck, because it was honest. No uniforms, no chain of command, no pretending the old world was coming back. Just a syndicate of the stubborn: scavengers, mechanics, ex-cons and line cooks, running convoys by day and holding jukebox bars by night, paying each other in scrip stamped G$ because somebody with a sense of humor looted a mint's worth of blank checkbooks.

Then the obelisks started humming. Old stone, wrong angles, standing in fields nobody plowed. Stand close after you die — a new body, same debts — and the stone remembers you: your hands relearn the rifle, the wrench, the stitch. It costs. Everything in the county costs. But the Goons have an economy now, and an economy means the dead are just another market condition.

Bandit crews strip the highways. Sirens howl on Saturdays. Helicopters that answer to no one sweep the treeline. And in the middle of it all, the syndicate wires every member a stipend, prices every head that needs pricing, and keeps the lights on at the one bar in Kentucky where the end of the world takes requests.
